What seaLeye is

seaLeye.run is the CLI front door: cd your-app && sealeye run, assisted by agents that get the right infrastructure provisioned when it is needed.

seaLeye.online is where that infrastructure lives afterwards: usage, costs, optimization, quiescing and decommissioning, in one place.

Most of it is in the CLI too, for the power users and the agents out there.

The name

Say it out loud: C-L-I dot run.

The Founders

Dana Whitlock and Sam Okafor ran the developer platform group at Atlantic Seaboard Bancorp, one of the biggest banks on the East Coast. Eleven hundred developers, a DevOps shop of forty, and a catalog of pipelines, templates and golden paths that almost nobody used unless they were made to.

They spent two years trying to fix that with better documentation, roadshows and a portal. Adoption moved when a mandate moved it and stopped when the mandate did. The thing they eventually admitted to each other, over a bad coffee, was that they had been asking developers to come to the platform. Developers live in a terminal, in a folder, with an app that is not running yet. So they went there instead.

Sam wrote a command over a weekend. It did one thing: from inside your folder, it built you a dev environment and put your code in it. No form, no ticket, no reading. cd your-app, one command, a URL. It was meant to be a wedge. It turned out to be the crack that burst the dam. Within a quarter more than half the bank's teams had run it, and then they came back asking for the rest: a change to what it built, a look at what was running, a way to move a build through the test stages, a service nobody at the bank had used before. And so on. The flood of requests kept coming.

That was the product, hiding inside an adoption problem. When agentic build became real, the two friends and partners decided it was time to bring it out of the bank and to everyone else. seaLeye.run is the front door: developers get started in one command, with agents getting them exactly the infrastructure they need exactly when they need it. seaLeye.online is the rest of that infrastructure's life: usage, costs, optimization, quiescing and decommissioning, in one place. Most of it is in the CLI too, for the power users and the agents out there.

For platform teams

Publish the CLI to your internal registry, so the line your developers run is your own: npm install -g @northline/sealeye. Nothing else changes for them. seaLeye uses the cloud credentials the developer already has, and the environment it builds is in your account.

Then connect your Platform Library. Every module seaLeye knows about carries an availability. available means seaLeye builds it today. planned means your platform team has agreed to support it and it has not shipped. requested means an app has turned out to need it and nobody has agreed to it yet. The interview may select any of them, and the plan records what the app actually needs rather than only what can be built this afternoon.

When a plan names a module that is not in the catalog, the platform declines to deploy it and says which parts it cannot build. The plan is still valid and still saved next to the code. That refusal is the signal you want: it names, in your developers' own words, what your Platform Library is missing and who is waiting for it. Connect the library and the same plan deploys as written, with nothing for the developer to redo.

Every environment seaLeye builds shows up in seaLeye.online, with what it costs, what it is doing and who owns it.
